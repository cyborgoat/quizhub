## Interpret probabilities, not guarantees

A probability describes uncertainty about a defined event over a stated time horizon; it does not promise what will happen in one case. A base rate is the broader frequency that provides context for case-specific evidence. For diagnostic tests, sensitivity and specificity are not the same as the chance that a positive result is correct: that predictive value also depends on how common the condition is in the relevant population. Bayesian updating combines prior information with how well new evidence fits competing explanations.

## Distinguish likelihood, impact, and value

Expected value is a probability-weighted average across possible outcomes. It is useful for comparing repeated or uncertain outcomes, but it does not describe every feature of a single outcome or everyone’s preferences. Risk decisions may also depend on severity, extreme outcomes, who bears the harm, reversibility, and tolerance for uncertainty. A low-probability event can merit attention when its impact is serious.

## Communicate risk clearly

State the denominator, timeframe, and baseline when comparing risks. Relative changes can sound large or small depending on the starting risk, so give absolute values where possible. Natural frequencies can make conditional probabilities easier to follow. Forecasts should identify their reference class and assumptions; scenarios and sensitivity checks show how conclusions change when inputs vary. Confidence intervals and other uncertainty summaries require interpretation under their statistical assumptions rather than being treated as guarantees.
