## Match the process to the decision

Start by clarifying objectives, constraints, and what would make an option acceptable. The right amount of analysis depends on the stakes, uncertainty, and reversibility: a small, safe experiment can be a quick way to learn, while a consequential and hard-to-reverse commitment usually deserves more scrutiny. A pre-mortem—imagining the plan has failed and identifying plausible reasons—can expose risks early.

## Compare options without false precision

A decision matrix makes criteria and trade-offs visible, but its scores and weights reflect judgments rather than objective facts. Test whether a small change in assumptions would alter the ranking. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best use of limited time or resources. Expected value can summarize uncertain outcomes, but it does not by itself resolve questions about downside risk, fairness, or rights.

More information is useful only when it could change the choice enough to justify the cost or delay. A stopping rule can define when additional searching is unlikely to improve the decision. Prefer robust options that remain acceptable across plausible scenarios over an option that looks optimal under only one fragile forecast.

## Separate process quality from outcome

A good process cannot guarantee a good result when chance and uncertainty remain. Record assumptions, decision criteria, and expected outcomes before acting; then review results against those criteria rather than judging the original choice only by what happened. An implementation plan should name owners, measures, milestones, and conditions that would trigger adaptation.
